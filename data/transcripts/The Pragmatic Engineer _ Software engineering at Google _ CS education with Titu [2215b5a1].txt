Give it a spin In this episode An engineer who helped create today’s software engineering curriculum now says to burn down almost all of it. What’s the cause of this contradiction? We find out from the lead author of Software Engineering at Google . Titus Winters spent more than a decade at Google, where he led its readability program and built Abseil , Google’s open-sourced foundational C++ libraries. He is currently a Senior Principal Scientist at Adobe, where he focuses on developer experience and productivity. In this episode of The Pragmatic Engineer podcast , Titus and I discuss how his team tackled a massive refactoring project across Google’s 250-million-line C++ codebase, why Google made testing mandatory, and his work on updating the ACM/IEEE undergraduate computer science curriculum guidelines. We also explore why traditional productivity metrics miss some of AI’s biggest benefits, what needs to change in CS education, and how new engineers can stand out. Takeaways from the conversation with Titus Titus is a deep thinker and has worked on some very interesting projects. Here are 12 things that stick out to me from our chat. 1. Was refactoring 250M+ lines of C++ code the largest refactoring task of all time? This happened at Google in 2010, a few months after Titus joined the search giant. An internal email asked for volunteers to help clean up old C++ code, some of it written by the very first Googlers. A team of a dozen people was assembled. Google had close to 250M lines of code at the time, edited by 10,000 engineers. Titus recalled the cleanup: “We were replacing the foundation of the Empire State Building. We were not allowed to close the building or block traffic. Oh, and we discovered nuclear waste in the basement.” 2. The refactoring was done with advanced language parsing techniques, long before LLMs existed. Back in 2010, there were no LLMs for this giant refactor. Here’s what the team did: Parse : ran a MapReduce over the whole codebase. The mapper parsed every build target into an abstract syntax tree Match : match exact call patterns in the tree Code generation: generated edits to be made by the machine Coexist : make the old API and new API work side-by-side Shard : once the new API is ready to go, split the edits by directory, and send them out in batches Block the old: make sure nobody copied the old pattern back into the code Delete : remove the old API The clang-format toolkit exists because Titus and his team needed it for this refactor. 3. Google made automated tests mandatory after Search could no longer ship regularly. Around 2007, Search, the flagship product, was unable to ship at a monthly cadence any more, and the best they could ship to production was quarterly. Almost every change at Google touched Search’s code, even things like a change to Gmail. To clamp down, Google, as an org, mandated that every change was to be accompanied by a test. Over time, Search returned to its former higher cadence. 4. Google’s readability program might have been the largest educational program ever in tech. To make a code change at Google, two people need to sign off that change through a code review: An “owner” of the file/project: someone on the team with domain knowledge A “readability” reviewer: an engineer certified to be a “readability” expert in the given language that the change was made in Google’s first engineer, Craig Silverstein, walked each new dev hire through their first change. Java teams turned this approach into mentoring, where experienced reviewers were assigned to review all changes in a round-robin fashion. And soon enough, a formalized readability program was built for all languages. When Titus left Google three months ago, 10,000+ engineers were enrolled in readability at any given time. Has there ever been a bigger engineering “classroom” anywhere else? 5. Fear is not an acceptable basis for engineering. Titus believes your codebase is unsustainable if it’s not possible to make a valuable change safely. There shouldn’t be fear about making a change to a codebase. Having the right “security nets” that catch errors is strategically very valuable because it enables teams to make changes without fear. 6. With AI, outages seem to be going up. Titus talks to engineers at other companies and it’s the same picture: code output is up hugely versus a year ago, and outages are also up about 30%, while the number of features shipped is static. It seems like treading water. From that angle, it’s not positive for AI; based on outages alone, the technology could be a negative. 7. On the other hand, AI makes ambitious projects possible that weren’t realistic without it. Titus is optimistic about AI as a net productivity boost, and reckons people are caring about the wrong things. He sees the real value of AI as not in repeating the same things as before at higher speed. Instead, it’s about doing things you never thought of before: projects so ambitious that they didn’t used to make sense! 8. Burn down the US undergrad CS curriculum (most of it). Titus was the only working engineer on the steering committee for the ACM/IEEE/AAAI CS curriculum guidelines in 2023, and believes the curriculum is hopelessly out of date. He accuses it of not having altered substantially since around 2000, while the industry has. Testing and verification are cited as mattering a lot more for new joiners, but they are absent from the curriculum. Unless major changes are made to make it relevant, computer science enrollment could fall and software engineering programs that focus on practical dev skills will be attractive to students – this is Titus’s forecast. 9. “Middle group” of students struggle most with AI. Professors in CS programs tell Titus that students divide into three groups: The best are better than ever and take full advantage of a patient, knowledgeable tutor, aka the AI The middle: professors say that everyone below the “best” have collapsed: students below a B grade tend to not understand code at all The weakest: they can catch up to the middle of the pack easier – mostly by producing slop with AI, though. When I did a guest lecture at my alma mater, professors told me similar observations – how the “middle of the pack students,” by grades, have disappointingly low understanding of the subject, compared to former years. 10. Grading students’ prompts and tests is better than focusing on their code. Titus would focus on the thinking processes students put into building and validating software, not just the output. 11. “Remember Andrew’s axe” – advice for junior engineers. Andrew chops down a tree with an axe. Or did Andrew’s axe chop down the tree? Titus says Andrew is the one who did it, and the axe is only a tool for his will. Similarly with AI: it’s a tool for you to use, and anthropomorphizing this tool takes us out of that mindset. 12. To succeed, don’t copy what everyone else is doing! As our time came to an end, I asked Titus what he’d say to sophomore students. It’s a segment that did not make it into the podcast, but I’m adding it here! “Most of my success in life has come from not doing the thing that everyone else is doing. It is really incredibly hard to stand out if you are just listening to what is a hot topic and chasing the same shiny thing that literally everyone else is doing. My primary advice [is to] find something that you love that is not just the same thing that you assume is on everybody’s resume, and do a lot of that. Find a passion project, go really deep on it. Like, ‘how do I use computing and AI vibe coded new generation techniques?’ ‘How do I solve this problem that matters a lot to me?’ ‘Or how do I express myself through a game or art?’ The more that you do [something] that’s uniquely you , the more that you stand out, and the more that you do, the more that you learn .” The Pragmatic Engineer deepdives relevant for this episode • The state of the tech industry in 2026 • Inside Google’s Engineering Culture • Slow down to speed up when working with AI agents • Measuring developer productivity?
